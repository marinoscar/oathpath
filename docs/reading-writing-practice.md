# Practicing Reading and Writing

Your naturalization interview includes two more moments beyond the civics
questions: an officer will hand you a sentence to read aloud, and later
read you a different sentence to write down. OathPath lets you practice
both, the same way they actually happen in the interview.

This page explains what actually happens when you do — including one part
that is deliberately different from how the rest of OathPath works, and
why.

## Reading: you see it, and you hear it

When you practice reading, the sentence is shown on the screen, and you can
also have it read aloud before you try. You read it aloud yourself — by
speaking it, the same way you would practice a spoken civics answer — and
OathPath scores how closely what you said matches the sentence.

You do not need to get every word exactly right. **Missing or changing one
word is not a failure.** People drop a small word, or say "the" where the
sentence says nothing, all the time while reading correctly. OathPath only
counts it as not-reading when two or more words are actually wrong — one
slip on an otherwise correct reading still passes.

When something doesn't match, you'll see exactly which word — a highlighted
diff between what the sentence said and what you said — rather than just a
score. That's so a near-miss teaches you something specific, not just "try
again."

## Writing: you hear it, and only that

Writing works differently, on purpose: **the sentence is read to you, and
never shown on the screen before you write it.**

This might feel unusual the first time you hit it — everywhere else in
OathPath, and in most apps, you'd expect to see what you're being asked to
type. Here's why writing is the one place that's different:

The actual interview does not show you the sentence either. An officer
reads it to you, and you write down what you heard. If OathPath displayed
the sentence while you typed, you would be practicing something real but
different: copying visible text accurately, which is not the same skill as
turning spoken English into written English with nothing to look at. You
could type a perfect copy of a sentence you can see and still not be ready
for the interview — and OathPath would have told you that you were, which
would be worse than not testing you at all. Hiding the sentence is what
makes your score mean what it says it means.

If you'd like to hear it again before you finish, you can — replaying the
sentence as many times as you want never counts against you. Needing a
few replays isn't a mark against you; it's just information, and it costs
you nothing.

The same near-miss rule applies here too: one wrong word out of several is
not a failure, and you'll see the diff — and, for the first time, the
sentence itself — once you submit, so you can see exactly what you heard
correctly and what you didn't.

## Being misheard is not the same as being wrong

Reading practice uses your voice, which means it goes through the same
speech recognition every spoken practice in OathPath uses — and sometimes
that recognition gets something wrong that has nothing to do with whether
you actually read the sentence correctly.

When that happens and it's clear the transcription wasn't reliable,
OathPath does not record it as a missed sentence at all. Nothing is scored,
nothing counts against you, and you're simply asked to try again. Your
reading is measured by what you actually said — so if there's real reason
to doubt that what was heard is what you said, the honest thing to do is
record nothing, not to guess and hold it against you.

## Where to find it

Reading and writing practice are their own screens, reachable from Practice
— look for them alongside your other practice options.

If your device or installation doesn't offer voice input, reading practice
still works: instead of a microphone, you read the sentence aloud to
yourself and simply tell OathPath how it went — "I read it word for word"
or "I missed or changed a word." OathPath says plainly that nobody but you
checked that one, and if you say you missed a word, nothing is recorded at
all rather than counted against you — the same honest-absence choice as
being misheard. Either way, you're never blocked from practicing.

If your device can't speak a writing sentence aloud at all, OathPath tells
you so plainly and points you to reading practice instead — it never falls
back to showing you the writing sentence to copy. That would quietly turn
writing practice into copying practice, which is exactly the substitution
the rest of this page explains why OathPath won't make.
