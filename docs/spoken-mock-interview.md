# Practicing a Spoken Mock Interview

Your naturalization interview is a conversation with an officer, not a form
you fill in. OathPath's mock interview can be typed, and on some
installations it can also be spoken — a live, back-and-forth conversation
with an officer that asks questions the way the real interview does and lets
you answer the way you actually will: out loud, at your own pace,
interrupting and being interrupted the way any real conversation is.

This page explains what actually happens when you take one.

## OathPath decides the questions and the result — never the officer's own memory

The voice you hear sounds like it is having a conversation with you, and in
one sense it is: it decides what to say next and how to say it. But it never
decides **what** to ask or **whether you got it right**. Every civics
question you hear is pulled, word for word, from OathPath's own official
question bank — never invented, never paraphrased from what a model
happens to recall about the naturalization test. And whether your answer
counts as correct is decided the same way it is decided everywhere else in
OathPath: by checking what you said against the accepted answers, not by
the officer's own impression of how you sounded.

That distinction matters more here than almost anywhere else in this
product. A conversational voice that graded you by ear could, in principle,
be generous on a bad day and strict on a good one, with no way for you to
know why. OathPath does not work that way: replay the identical interview
twice and you get the identical questions and the identical verdict, because
neither one was ever the model's to decide.

## Being misheard is never recorded as a wrong answer

Speaking to a microphone during a rehearsal for one of the more important
moments in your life is already nerve-wracking; being marked wrong because
the microphone caught your accent strangely or dropped a word in a noisy
room would make it worse for no good reason. So when OathPath's own
confidence in what it heard is low, and your answer would otherwise have
counted as incorrect, it is recorded as a possible mishearing, not as a
wrong answer — the identical protection [spoken practice](spoken-practice.md)
already gives you outside of a mock interview, extended here to the
interview itself. Your progress reflects what you know, not how clearly a
microphone happened to pick you up during one conversation.

## You can end the interview at any moment

The control to end the interview is always there, and it is never disabled
— not while the officer is mid-sentence, not while you are answering, not at
any point in the conversation. If you need to stop, you stop, and OathPath
records exactly how far you got, never more than actually happened.

## Typing is never taken away

Every mock interview you can take by voice, you can also take by typing —
there is no version of this feature that requires speaking. If your
microphone is unavailable, if you would simply rather type, or if something
about the connection does not cooperate, the interview continues in text
with nothing lost: the same questions, the same officer, the same result at
the end. You are never stuck, and you are never penalized for choosing to
type instead of speak.

## Keeping a transcript is your choice, and it starts off

Before a mock interview begins, you are asked once whether you would like
OathPath to keep a transcript of what you said during it. **The default is
off**, and it stays off unless you turn it on for that interview. Choosing
"off" does not make the interview count for less — your questions, your
accepted answers, and how you did are kept either way, exactly as they
would be for a typed interview. What "off" removes is only your own
phrasing: the specific words you used are not retained for you to review
later.

## Your voice is never stored, on any version of the interview

Whichever way you take it, and whatever you chose about keeping a
transcript, **the recording of your voice itself is never saved** — not to
look back on later, not for anyone at OathPath to listen to, not anywhere on
your device. What might be kept, if you chose to, is the text of what you
said. The sound of you saying it is never a thing this product retains,
because a rehearsal for your citizenship interview is not something OathPath
has any reason to keep a recording of.

## Where to find it

Look for a "practice by voice" option when starting a mock interview. If you
don't see one, spoken mock interviews simply are not available on this
installation of OathPath yet — the typed mock interview works exactly the
same either way, with nothing else about your practice affected. See also
[Practicing With Your Voice](spoken-practice.md) for how spoken civics
practice outside of a mock interview works, and
[Practicing Reading and Writing](reading-writing-practice.md) for the
reading and writing portions a spoken mock interview conducts for real,
rather than skipping.
